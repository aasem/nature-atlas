The Governing Question

What is nature, where do we stand in it, and what may we do to it?

Every picture of nature contains a picture of the one who knows it, and the two are drawn at the same time. Plato’s cosmos is a living creature, so the human soul is a small copy of the world-soul and knows the heavens by contemplating them. Descartes’ nature is extension and nothing else, so the mind that knows it must be something other than nature, and it can become nature’s master and possessor. For Crowley nature is the field in which the will acts, so the human being is a will. The question has three clauses because each answer decides the next.

The atlas began with a sentence often attributed to Francis Bacon, that nature must be tortured to make her reveal her secrets. Bacon did not write it. He wrote that nature shows herself more fully when vexed and constrained by art than when left free, and he took his image from Proteus, who prophesies only when seized and held. The misquotation has lasted because it states plainly what his programme implied. The entries follow the attitude rather than the phrase, across twelve traditions, from the Presocratics to the machines that now answer us.

The middle clause carries the question of human nature. Where a tradition places the human being, within nature, over it or apart from it, its account of the soul, the mind and consciousness follows. Those who stand apart make the mind immaterial and nature mindless. Those who stand within make the mind a part of nature, either as something that emerges from matter or as something present in matter from the start. Those who stand over nature tend to define the self as a will. Each entry therefore asks what a text says about the human being as well as what it says about the world.

The Traditions

A short account of each tradition’s picture of nature and of the human being, so that the entries can be read in context.

Greece and RomeThe Growing World

The Greek word physis comes from a verb meaning to grow, and the first inquirers into nature were asking how things come to be and keep themselves in being. For almost all of them the cosmos was alive. Plato made it a single ensouled creature shaped by a divine craftsman, Aristotle made nature an inner principle by which each thing moves toward its end, and the Stoics filled the whole with a fiery, rational breath. The human being was a part of this order, distinguished by reason within it, and the right response to nature was to imitate it. Lucretius is the great exception: nothing exists but atoms and void, and nature is creative without a creator. Even he opens his poem with a hymn to Venus. Plotinus completes the ancient line by making nature the last and faintest phase of contemplation, a soul that produces bodies in silence.

ChinaThings So of Themselves

The modern Chinese word for nature, ziran, comes from the Daodejing, where it means what is so of itself, spontaneity rather than a realm of things. Chinese thought is often called organic, and Joseph Needham argued that it pictured a cosmos of patterned relations without a lawgiver. The Daoists counsel non-interference and fear the ‘machine heart’ that tools produce. The correlative cosmology of the Han binds body, state and cosmos in a web of resonances, and Neo-Confucianism explains everything as principle (li) and vital stuff (qi), so that knowing things and knowing oneself are one task. The picture is not uniform. Xunzi asks why one should revere heaven when one could domesticate it, and the Mohists measured and engineered. What the tradition never did was separate the principle of things from their moral meaning, and that, more than any lack of method, may explain why it did not take the Baconian path.

IndiaWitness and Dancer

India produced the most precise pre-modern anticipation of Cartesian dualism and drew the opposite conclusion from it. In Sāṃkhya, nature (prakṛti) is a single productive principle that includes intellect and mind; consciousness belongs only to the inactive witness (puruṣa), which nature serves like a dancer who stops when she has been seen. The aim is not mastery of nature but release from fascination with it. Around this stand other answers. The Vedic hymns see a cosmic order (ṛta) sustained by sacrifice; the Upaniṣads find brahman in every element; Śaṅkara calls the world an appearance; Abhinavagupta calls it the real self-expression of consciousness. Vaiśeṣika and the Cārvākas built atomisms and materialisms that never became programmes of control, and the Jains extended non-injury to earth, water and fire. In the modern period J. C. Bose tried to prove Upaniṣadic unity with laboratory instruments, Tagore opposed the forest hermitage to the walled city, and Gandhi made Zhuangzi’s refusal of the machine into politics.

IslamSigns and the Trust

The Qurʾān calls natural phenomena signs (āyāt), the same word it uses for its own verses, so that nature and scripture are two texts by one author. It also says that the heavens and earth have been made subservient to humans, and that the human took on a trust that the heavens, earth and mountains refused. The tradition’s quarrels turn on these ideas. The Ikhwān al-Ṣafāʾ let the animals sue humanity for cruelty. Ibn Sīnā’s flying man knows itself without its body, six centuries before Descartes. Al-Ghazālī denied that natural things have power of their own and Ibn Rushd replied that a thing without powers has no definition. Ibn Ṭufayl’s solitary islander derived an ecological ethic from cosmology, and Ibn ʿArabī and Mullā Ṣadrā made nature the self-disclosure of being in perpetual motion. In South Asia, Sayyid Ahmad Khan and Iqbal met modern science and, in different ways, accepted its conquest of nature as compatible with the Qurʾān.

The Bible and ChristendomThe Two Books

Genesis gives humans dominion over the creatures and places Adam in the garden to till and keep it. Job answers with the voice from the whirlwind, which shows creatures that serve no human purpose. Medieval Christianity read nature as a second book written by God, and the book was meant for the reader’s conversion. Augustine distinguished the order of being, in which a living mouse outranks a gem, from the order of use, in which we prefer the gem. Maimonides insisted that the universe was not made for humanity. Hildegard saw the elements crying out against human sin, Francis called fire a brother, and Aquinas gave every creature its own nature and end while ordering all of them to humans. Lynn White’s claim that this tradition caused the ecological crisis has to be weighed against its own internal critics, who are among the sharpest in the atlas.

The RenaissanceThe Magus and the Living Cosmos

The Renaissance recovered both the Hermetic texts and Lucretius, and for a century the living cosmos and the atomist one coexisted. Ficino’s world has a soul and a spirit through which stars reach stones and herbs, and his magus marries heaven to earth like a farmer. Pico’s Adam has no fixed nature at all and can make himself whatever he chooses, which sets him apart from every creature that has one. Paracelsus walked the book of nature with his feet, Telesio explained nature by its own principles, Bruno made the universe infinite and alive, and Campanella said that stones feel. Montaigne, meanwhile, asked whether his cat was playing with him or he with her, and called human presumption our original disease. Most of the new philosophy was written against this world, and much of it was borrowed from it.

The New PhilosophyMasters and Possessors

Between Bacon’s New Organon of 1620 and Locke’s Essay of 1690 the picture changed. Nature lost its ends, its souls and its qualities. Bacon made knowledge and power one, and asked for an empire of man over things that would recover what was lost in Eden. Galileo moved colour, taste and warmth out of the world into the perceiving mind, and Descartes made the mind a thinking thing really distinct from an extended, mechanical nature, which it might master and possess. Animals became machines, and the human body too. Hobbes refused the exemption and made the whole human a machine. Boyle, out of piety, proposed to abolish the word nature. Against them stood Cavendish and Conway, who held that all matter perceives, the Cambridge Platonists with their plastic nature, Spinoza with his single God or Nature, Pascal terrified by infinite spaces, and Leibniz, whose mill argument still states the problem of consciousness. Locke’s settlement, in which nature in itself has neither colour nor value until labour mixes with it, is the one the following centuries inherited.

Mechanism and Its Critics, 1700–1900The Clock and the Blade of Grass

The eighteenth and nineteenth centuries completed the machine and rebelled against it at once. La Mettrie made the human a machine and concluded that we should be kind. Hume removed necessity from causation, and his Philo suggested that the world resembles a vegetable more than a clock. Kant restated Bacon’s interrogation of nature as a judge compelling witnesses, then wrote that there would never be a Newton of a blade of grass. Goethe, Schelling, Blake and Wordsworth answered the mechanical picture with a living nature known by a transformed observer, and Humboldt saw the climatic effects of deforestation in 1800. Darwin returned the human being to nature without exemption. Mill denied that nature could be a moral guide, Marx called labour a metabolism with nature that capitalism disrupts, and Nietzsche rejected both the organism and the machine while calling our attitude to nature hubris.

The OccultCorrespondence and Will

The occult tradition runs alongside every other section of the atlas, from the Hermetic texts of Roman Egypt to chaos magic. Its oldest principle is correspondence, as above so below: the cosmos is a living web of sympathies, and the human being is a small cosmos who can act on the large one because he is made like it. Its oldest practices, Iamblichus’ theurgy and Ficino’s astral medicine, were forms of receptivity. But the tradition also contained, from the Jābirian dream of generating life in a flask to Agrippa’s magic as the perfection of natural philosophy, an ambition of operation that Frances Yates believed passed into modern science. From Éliphas Lévi onward the emphasis moved to the will, and Crowley’s definition of magick as change in conformity with will is Bacon’s programme stripped of theology. Dion Fortune then moved magic’s object from nature to consciousness, conceding the outer world to science. The twentieth century also produced the occult’s returns to a sacred nature: Steiner’s farm as an organism and the Wiccan wheel of the year.

The PerennialistsThe Reign of Quantity

The Traditionalist school, founded by René Guénon, holds that the world’s religions share a single metaphysical doctrine and that modernity is a fall away from it. For them modern science is not a discovery but a symptom: the reduction of quality to quantity that Galileo began. Coomaraswamy defined traditional art as imitating nature in her manner of operation, Schuon called virgin nature a sanctuary, Burckhardt read alchemy as a science of the soul, and Seyyed Hossein Nasr diagnosed the environmental crisis as a spiritual one, the work of Promethean man who has forgotten that he is pontifical, a bridge between heaven and earth. The section includes Julius Evola as a warning that a critique of modern science and a sacred view of nature can be joined to a politics of domination.

Twentieth-Century ReckoningsStanding-Reserve

In the twentieth century the Baconian project was judged by its results. Weber named the belief that everything could in principle be mastered by calculation the disenchantment of the world. Husserl accused Galileo of mistaking a garment of formulas for the world, and Whitehead called the result the bifurcation of nature. Heidegger argued that modern technology reveals nature as standing-reserve, stock on call, and Horkheimer and Adorno that domination of nature becomes domination of people. C. S. Lewis made the same point for schoolteachers. Merleau-Ponty answered Descartes with a hand touching the other hand. Rachel Carson called the control of nature an arrogant phrase, Jonas proposed an imperative of responsibility for a power that reaches the future, Lynn White blamed Christianity, and Carolyn Merchant blamed the mechanical philosophy and the gendered language of Bacon.

The Contemporary SceneAfter Nature

Recent thought has two broad responses. One returns agency, value or mind to the rest of nature: deep ecology’s intrinsic value, Gaia’s self-regulating planet, the new materialists’ lively matter, Nagel’s natural teleology and the revived panpsychists, who call their opponent ‘Galileo’s error’. The other questions the category of nature itself. Descola shows that the modern division between a single physical nature and human minds is one of four ontologies, Viveiros de Castro that Amazonian thought reverses it, and Latour that the moderns never actually kept the two apart. Chakrabarty adds that humans are now a geological force, and Yuk Hui that every technology carries a cosmology. Kimmerer offers the grammar of animacy, and Pope Francis the critique of a technocratic paradigm. At the far end stand the transhumanists, who treat human nature itself as raw material, and the machines that now pass the tests Descartes set to separate us from automata.

Across the Traditions

The second pass. Each of the questions in turn, with the traditions set against one another.

Nature

Seven pictures of nature recur. Nature is alive for Plato, the Stoics, Ficino, Bruno, Cavendish, Conway, Goethe, Schelling, Bose and the Gaia theorists, and for the Jains even earth and fire are bodies of souls. It is an ordered whole for Aristotle, the Daoists, Zhu Xi, Kepler, Spinoza and Kant, an order in which things have their places and their patterns. It is a book of signs for the Qurʾān, Augustine, Bonaventure, Ibn ʿArabī, Boehme, Swedenborg and the perennialists, a text that points beyond itself. It is a veil for Plotinus, Sāṃkhya, Yoga and Śaṅkara, to be seen through rather than read, and, oddly, for Harman’s withdrawn objects. It is a web of relations for Zhuangzi, the Huainanzi, the Buddha, Nāgārjuna, Darwin, Whitehead and most contemporary thought. It is a machine for Lucretius in one sense and for Descartes, Hobbes, Boyle and La Mettrie in another. And it is a store of power for Bacon, Crowley and the transhumanists, and for those who diagnose that picture, from Weber and Heidegger to Merchant.

The most surprising agreements cross traditions. The Vaiśeṣika atomists, the Cārvākas and Lucretius all built mechanical natures that never became programmes of mastery, which shows that mechanism alone does not produce conquest. Al-Ghazālī, Malebranche and Hume, from three religious positions, all denied that observation reveals necessary connection. Hildegard, the Huainanzi and the Caraka Saṃhitā all linked natural disaster to human disorder. And the microcosm, the human being as a small copy of the world, appears in Plato, the Ikhwān al-Ṣafāʾ, Dong Zhongshu, Caraka, Hildegard, Paracelsus and Fludd before the seventeenth century dissolved it.

The Human

Three stances recur, and each carries its own theory of mind. Those who stand within nature, which is most of the texts in every period, treat the mind as a part of nature: as a finer matter (the Stoics, Lucretius, the Cārvākas, Hobbes), as a form of the living body (Aristotle, Aquinas, Mullā Ṣadrā), as nature becoming conscious of itself (Schelling, Aurobindo, Teilhard) or as one perceiver among countless others (Cavendish, Leibniz, the panpsychists). Those who stand apart make consciousness a witness that does not belong to the world it sees: Sāṃkhya, Yoga, Śaṅkara, Ibn Sīnā’s flying man, Descartes and Malebranche. Those who stand over nature define the self by its will or its office: Xunzi’s triad with heaven and earth, the Qurʾānic vicegerent, Pico’s Adam, Bacon’s empire, Crowley’s star and Bostrom’s posthuman.

The deepest pair in the atlas is Sāṃkhya and Descartes. Both split the person in two. Sāṃkhya puts thinking, willing and feeling on the side of nature and leaves consciousness alone apart, a witness that does nothing. Descartes puts thinking on the side of the soul and gives it dominion over an extended world. The first leads to renunciation and the second to mastery. Every later quarrel about consciousness, from Elisabeth’s letter to Leibniz’s mill to Chalmers’ hard problem, is a quarrel about where the line falls.

Knowing

Five ways of knowing nature recur. Contemplation (Plato, Plotinus, the Neo-Confucians) watches nature and is changed by watching. Reading (the Qurʾān, Bonaventure, Paracelsus, Swedenborg) interprets signs. Experiment comes in two kinds: Bacon’s and Kant’s, which constrain nature and interrogate it like a witness, and Goethe’s delicate empiricism, which trains the observer until he sees. Initiation (Iamblichus, the Golden Dawn, Gurdjieff) transforms the knower so that he can perceive what is hidden. Participation (Zhuangzi’s cook, Merleau-Ponty’s touching hand, Kimmerer’s grammar of animacy) knows from inside the relation.

The critics of instruments form an unexpected line: Zhuangzi’s gardener, Cavendish on the microscope, Goethe on Newton’s prism, Husserl on the garb of ideas. None of them denied that instruments work. Each argued that the instrument changes what is known and the one who knows it, which is the point Heidegger finally generalized.

What May Be Done

The question of limits divides the atlas more sharply than any other. Most pre-modern traditions set limits in the nature of things: the seasonal ordinances of the Huainanzi, the Jain ethic of non-injury, the Qurʾānic balance, Ḥayy ibn Yaqẓān’s rules for eating. They also contain ambitions without limit, Xunzi’s domesticated heaven, the Jābirian generation of life, Pico’s Adam, and Bacon did not invent the dream of mastery. What changed in the seventeenth century was that the dream acquired a method and an institution, and the limit disappeared from the theory. The effecting of all things possible, in Bacon’s phrase, has no limit in it.

The modern attempts to put a limit back fall into four groups. Some restore a sacred nature (the perennialists, Laudato si’, Wicca). Some restore value to nature without the sacred (Naess, Jonas, Carson). Some argue that the domination of nature is really the domination of people (Lewis, Horkheimer and Adorno, Marx, Merchant). And some change the category of nature itself (Descola, Latour, Chakrabarty). Kabbalah’s tiqqun, repair of a broken world, is the oldest version of a fifth possibility, a human role larger than stewardship and different from mastery.

The Recurring Quarrels

The same disagreements reappear across centuries and traditions. Does nature have powers of its own, or are its regularities only the habit of God (Ibn Rushd against al-Ghazālī, Leibniz against Malebranche and Boyle)? Is the cosmos alive or a clock (Fludd and Cavendish against Kepler’s letter and Boyle, Schelling against Newton’s followers)? Is the mind part of nature or outside it (Hobbes against Descartes, Conway against both, Sāṃkhya against the Cārvākas)? May the powers of nature be seized, or only received (Crowley against Iamblichus, Bacon against Ficino)? Is nature a moral teacher (the Stoics and Rousseau against Mill)? And is the crisis caused by religion, science or economics (White, Merchant and Marx against one another)? Knowing which of these quarrels a text belongs to is often the quickest way to understand it.

What Remains Unanswered

No tradition has explained how consciousness belongs to nature. Those who place it apart cannot explain how it acts on the body, as Elisabeth asked Descartes in 1643. Those who derive it from matter have no account of why matter should feel, as Leibniz’s mill shows. Those who place it everywhere cannot say how small experiences combine into a single self. The question was made acute by Galileo’s decision to leave qualities out of physics, and every answer since has either accepted that decision or tried to reverse it.

No tradition has a settled answer to the question of limits once human power reaches the whole planet and the far future. The pre-modern limits were set in a cosmos that humans could not damage, and Jonas is right that ethics never needed to think about the distant consequences of human action because those consequences could not occur. The limits now proposed, sacred, ecological or political, each presuppose a community that agrees to observe them.

It is not settled whether the mechanical picture caused the domination of nature or only made it easier. Pre-modern atomisms produced no programme of mastery, and pre-modern living cosmologies contained ambitions of total control. The occult evidence in this atlas suggests that the will to operate came first and found its instrument later, but Yates’ critics may be right that the connection is looser than she claimed.

And the last entry reopens the first question in a new form. Descartes separated humans from animals by their capacity to answer whatever is said to them, and machines now answer. If the machine has a mind, the mechanical picture has produced its own refutation. If it does not, then we still do not know what we are adding when we say a person has one. Either way, the place of the human in nature is less settled now than it was in 1620.
